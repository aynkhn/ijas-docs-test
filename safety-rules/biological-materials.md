# Use and Care of Biological Materials (Excluding Vertebrate Animals)
{: .no_toc }

1. TOC
{:toc}

## Why Do We Have These Rules?

Biological materials play an important role in medicine, agriculture, environmental science, biotechnology, and many other fields of research. When handled properly, microorganisms, cultured cells, environmental samples, algae, fungi, and non-vertebrate organisms can be studied safely and provide valuable scientific information. However, some biological materials can cause disease, produce harmful substances, or negatively impact the environment if handled or disposed of improperly.

{: .warning }
> Projects that do not follow these rules may be disqualified from the IJAS Science Fair.

## Does My Project Involve Biological Materials?

These rules apply if your project involves:

- bacteria, archaea, fungi, yeast, molds, protists, algae, or viruses;
- culturing or growing microorganisms;
- fermentation using microorganisms;
- microbial fuel cells;
- recombinant DNA techniques or genetically modified microorganisms;
- cultured cells or tissues;
- environmental samples such as soil, compost, wastewater, or natural water sources;
- biological specimens, including blood, tissues, or body fluids;
- non-vertebrate organisms, including insects, worms, crustaceans, mollusks, Daphnia, brine shrimp, planaria, hydra, or *C. elegans*; or
- any other project involving biological materials other than live vertebrate animals.

{: .note }
> If unsure whether these rules apply, contact your teacher or Regional Safety Chair first. If your project involves human participants or vertebrate animals, read the separate rules for those projects instead.

## Before You Begin Your Project

- read these Biological Materials Safety Rules with your sponsor;
- ensure appropriate adult supervision and an appropriate laboratory or research setting;
- learn proper laboratory techniques and biosafety procedures;
- identify the biological materials that will be used and understand their associated hazards;
- develop procedures for the safe handling, storage, and disposal of all biological materials;
- prepare a written experimental procedure;
- obtain SRC approval if required;
- complete the Biological Materials Endorsement Form; and
- not begin your project until all required paperwork and approvals have been completed.

## General Safety Requirements

Students working with biological materials must:

- wear appropriate PPE, including eye protection whenever appropriate;
- wear protective clothing and gloves when required;
- wash hands thoroughly before and after handling biological materials;
- use proper aseptic (sterile) technique whenever appropriate;
- disinfect work surfaces before and after experimentation;
- clearly label all cultures, biological materials, and containers;
- safely store biological materials to prevent contamination or accidental release;
- follow all laboratory safety procedures established by the school or research facility;
- properly disinfect biological cultures before disposal (e.g. autoclaving or an appropriate bleach solution); and
- immediately report spills, contamination, injuries, or unsafe conditions to the supervising adult.

## Working with Microorganisms

Projects involving bacteria, archaea, fungi, yeast, molds, protists, algae, or viruses must:

- use only non-pathogenic microorganisms obtained from reputable commercial suppliers, educational institutions, or research laboratories;
- document the source of all microorganisms with the endorsement form, research paper, and project display;
- be conducted in a school laboratory or professional research facility, unless the project uses only commercially obtained baker's yeast (*Saccharomyces cerevisiae*);
- use only sealed microbial fuel cell systems; and
- observe mold growth studies only until visible growth occurs — once visible, cultures must remain sealed and be properly disinfected and disposed of without further manipulation.

{: .note }
> If alcohol is produced as a natural byproduct of yeast fermentation, it may not be distilled, concentrated, purified, or consumed.

## Non-Vertebrate Organisms

Projects must:

- minimize unnecessary injury, stress, or harm to living organisms;
- provide appropriate housing, food, water, and environmental conditions whenever organisms are maintained;
- prevent accidental escape or release of organisms into the environment;
- comply with regulations governing invasive or regulated species; and
- properly dispose of organisms at the conclusion of the project.

## Not Allowed

Unless conducted in an appropriately approved research laboratory under qualified supervision, students may **not**:

- culture microorganisms collected from people or other vertebrate animals (mouth, nose, throat, skin, blood, body fluids, tissues, or waste);
- culture microorganisms collected from objects/surfaces that have contacted people or vertebrate animals (doorknobs, keyboards, phones, countertops, utensils, restroom surfaces);
- culture or intentionally propagate unknown microorganisms from environmental samples;
- conduct experiments using known disease-causing microorganisms;
- perform virus research;
- conduct recombinant DNA experiments;
- culture mammalian cells;
- intentionally release lab organisms or biological materials into the environment;
- distill, concentrate, or purify products of microorganism experiments when doing so creates additional hazards or violates laws/policies; or
- conduct biological materials research in locations not permitted by these rules.

## Documentation

If research is conducted at a certified laboratory or research facility, students must include a letter from the laboratory director or supervising scientist verifying:

- the research was conducted under appropriate adult supervision;
- the student followed all required laboratory safety procedures;
- the laboratory is certified or otherwise authorized to conduct the research;
- the supervising scientist's name, title, and contact information; and
- that the work described in the project was completed at that facility.
